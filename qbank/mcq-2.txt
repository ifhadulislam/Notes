@ch 07
Q: MHC class I molecules present antigen to:
A) CD4+ T cells
B) CD8+ T cells
C) B cells
D) NK cells (activating)
ANS: B
EX: Class I (HLA-A, B, C) presents cytosolic peptides to CD8+ T cells. Class II presents to CD4+ T cells. NK cells are inhibited by MHC-I.

Q: Goodpasture syndrome is an example of which hypersensitivity?
A) Type I
B) Type II
C) Type III
D) Type IV
ANS: B
EX: Anti-GBM antibodies against the α3 chain of type IV collagen produce linear IgG deposits. This is antibody against a fixed tissue antigen, i.e., type II.

Q: Arthus reaction is a:
A) Type I hypersensitivity
B) Localized type III reaction
C) Type IV reaction
D) Type II reaction
ANS: B
EX: The Arthus reaction is a local immune-complex vasculitis with fibrinoid necrosis at the injection site.

Q: Tuberculin test is an example of:
A) Type I
B) Type II
C) Type III
D) Type IV
ANS: D
EX: The tuberculin test is CD4+ Th1 delayed-type hypersensitivity, read at 48–72 h.

Q: Anti-histone antibodies are characteristic of:
A) SLE
B) Drug-induced lupus
C) Scleroderma
D) MCTD
ANS: B
EX: Hydralazine, procainamide and isoniazid cause drug-induced lupus with anti-histone antibodies. Renal and CNS involvement is rare.

Q: The most common and most severe form of lupus nephritis is:
A) Class II
B) Class III
C) Class IV
D) Class V
ANS: C
EX: Diffuse proliferative (class IV) shows wire-loop subendothelial deposits and full-house immunofluorescence.

Q: Anti-centromere antibodies are characteristic of:
A) Diffuse systemic sclerosis
B) Limited scleroderma (CREST)
C) Sjögren syndrome
D) Polymyositis
ANS: B
EX: Anti-centromere antibodies are seen in CREST. Anti-Scl-70 (topoisomerase I) is seen in diffuse SSc with lung fibrosis.

Q: Hyperacute rejection is mediated by:
A) CD8+ T cells
B) Pre-formed antibodies
C) NK cells
D) Macrophages
ANS: B
EX: Pre-formed anti-ABO or anti-HLA antibodies cause thrombosis within minutes. A pre-transplant cross-match prevents it.

Q: C4d deposition in peritubular capillaries is a marker of:
A) Acute cellular rejection
B) Antibody-mediated rejection
C) Calcineurin toxicity
D) CMV infection
ANS: B
EX: C4d is a complement split product that marks humoral (antibody-mediated) rejection.

Q: A 9-month-old boy has recurrent pyogenic infections, absent tonsils and very low levels of all immunoglobulins with no circulating B cells. The defect is in:
A) CD40 ligand
B) BTK
C) ADA
D) WAS
ANS: B
EX: X-linked agammaglobulinaemia (Bruton) is a BTK defect causing arrest at the pre-B stage. Symptoms start after 6 months, once maternal IgG wanes.

Q: The most common primary immunodeficiency is:
A) CVID
B) Selective IgA deficiency
C) DiGeorge syndrome
D) SCID
ANS: B
EX: Selective IgA deficiency occurs in about 1:700. Patients risk anaphylaxis with transfusion because of anti-IgA antibodies.

Q: Thrombocytopenia, eczema and recurrent infections in a boy suggest:
A) Ataxia telangiectasia
B) Wiskott–Aldrich syndrome
C) Chédiak–Higashi
D) DiGeorge
ANS: B
EX: Wiskott–Aldrich is an X-linked WAS defect with small platelets, low IgM, high IgA/IgE and lymphoma risk.

Q: HIV uses which co-receptor during early infection (macrophage-tropic strains)?
A) CXCR4
B) CCR5
C) CD8
D) CD21
ANS: B
EX: R5 strains use CCR5 early and are transmitted. X4 strains (CXCR4) emerge late. A homozygous CCR5Δ32 confers resistance.

Q: Earliest marker of HIV infection detectable in blood is:
A) Anti-gp41 antibody
B) p24 antigen / HIV RNA
C) CD4 decline
D) Western blot
ANS: B
EX: HIV RNA (by NAT) and p24 antigen appear before antibodies. Fourth-generation ELISA detects p24, which shortens the window period.

Q: CMV retinitis in AIDS typically occurs when CD4 count falls below:
A) 500/µL
B) 200/µL
C) 100/µL
D) 50/µL
ANS: D
EX: CMV retinitis and MAC occur below 50/µL, toxoplasmosis and cryptococcus below 100/µL, and PCP below 200/µL.

Q: Post-exposure prophylaxis for HIV should ideally be started within:
A) 2 hours (up to 72 h)
B) 1 week
C) 2 weeks
D) 1 month
ANS: A
EX: Start PEP as early as possible, ideally within 2 hours and no later than 72 hours. Give TDF + 3TC + DTG for 28 days.

Q: FOXP3 mutation causes:
A) ALPS
B) IPEX
C) APECED
D) Hyper-IgE syndrome
ANS: B
EX: Loss of Treg cells (FOXP3) causes immune dysregulation, polyendocrinopathy and enteropathy (X-linked IPEX). AIRE defects cause APS-1 (APECED).

A) Tuberculoid leprosy
B) Lepromatous leprosy
C) Indeterminate (always positive)

ANS: B

Q: Ulcers in typhoid are:
A) Transverse

C) Flask-shaped
D) Aphthous in colon
ANS: B
EX: Typhoid ulcers lie along the long axis of the ileum over Peyer patches. TB ulcers are transverse (girdle); amoebic ulcers are flask-shaped.

Q: The best investigation in the first week of typhoid fever is:
A) Widal test
B) Blood culture
C) Stool culture
D) Urine culture
ANS: B
EX: Blood culture is best in week 1 (bone marrow culture is the most sensitive). Widal from week 2, stool in week 3 and urine in week 4.

Q: Negri bodies are found in:
A) Anterior horn cells
B) Purkinje cells and hippocampal pyramidal neurons
C) Oligodendrocytes
D) Astrocytes
ANS: B
EX: Rabies causes eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusions in Purkinje cells and pyramidal neurons of Ammon's horn.

Q: Owl-eye intranuclear inclusions with cytomegaly are characteristic of:
A) HSV
B) CMV
C) Adenovirus
D) HPV
ANS: B
EX: CMV produces cytomegaly with owl-eye intranuclear inclusions plus cytoplasmic inclusions.

Q: Broad, ribbon-like aseptate hyphae branching at right angles in a diabetic with ketoacidosis indicate:
A) Aspergillus
B) Mucor
C) Candida
D) Cryptococcus
ANS: B
EX: Mucormycosis (Rhizopus/Mucor) is angioinvasive and causes rhino-orbito-cerebral disease in DKA. Aspergillus has narrow septate hyphae at acute angles.

Q: The most common cause of acquired epilepsy in India is:
A) Tuberculoma
B) Neurocysticercosis
C) Brain abscess
D) Toxoplasmosis
ANS: B
EX: Taenia solium cysticercosis (cysts with scolex, starry-sky CT) is the leading cause.

Q: Flask-shaped ulcers with trophozoites containing ingested RBCs are seen in:
A) Shigellosis
B) Amoebic colitis
C) Typhoid
D) Ulcerative colitis
ANS: B
EX: Entamoeba histolytica causes flask-shaped ulcers in the caecum and ascending colon. Erythrophagocytosis by trophozoites is diagnostic.

Q: Laminated, acellular, PAS-positive membrane with an inner germinal layer and brood capsules is seen in:
A) Cysticercus
B) Hydatid cyst
C) Amoebic abscess
D) Toxoplasma cyst
ANS: B
EX: The Echinococcus granulosus hydatid cyst has an outer laminated layer, an inner germinal layer and protoscolices ("hydatid sand").

Q: Hutchinson triad in congenital syphilis includes all EXCEPT:
A) Notched incisors
B) Interstitial keratitis
C) 8th nerve deafness
D) Saddle nose
ANS: D
EX: The Hutchinson triad is notched incisors, interstitial keratitis and deafness. Saddle nose is another late sign but is not part of the triad.

Q: Which is a cytoplasmic viral inclusion?
A) Cowdry A (HSV)
B) Guarnieri body
C) Smudge cell (adenovirus)
D) Owl-eye nuclear (CMV)
ANS: B
EX: Guarnieri bodies (poxvirus), Negri bodies and molluscum bodies are cytoplasmic. Herpes and adenovirus inclusions are nuclear.

A) Marfan syndrome

C) Ehlers–Danlos
D) Weill–Marchesani
ANS: B
EX: Homocystinuria gives downward and inward subluxation. Marfan gives upward and outward.

C) Foamy vacuolated cytoplasm with cherry red spot
D) Globoid cells

EX: Glucocerebroside fills macrophages and gives a "wrinkled silk" look. Gaucher disease is the commonest lysosomal storage disease.

Q: Hunter syndrome differs from Hurler syndrome by:
A) Corneal clouding present
B) X-linked inheritance and absent corneal clouding
C) Absent hepatosplenomegaly
D) Normal intelligence always
ANS: B
EX: Remember "Hunters aim for the X and see clearly". Hunter (MPS II) is X-linked and has no corneal clouding.

Q: McArdle disease is due to deficiency of:
A) Glucose-6-phosphatase
B) Muscle glycogen phosphorylase
C) Acid maltase
D) Debranching enzyme
ANS: B
EX: GSD V causes exercise cramps and myoglobinuria. Lactate does not rise on the ischaemic forearm test.

Q: Anticipation in fragile X syndrome occurs during transmission from:
A) Father
B) Mother
C) Both equally
D) Neither
ANS: B
EX: CGG expansion occurs during maternal transmission (fragile X). In Huntington disease, anticipation is paternal.

Q: The most common cause of Down syndrome is:
A) Robertsonian translocation
B) Meiotic non-disjunction
C) Mosaicism
D) Isochromosome 21
ANS: B
EX: About 95% is meiotic non-disjunction (maternal, age-related). Around 4% is due to Robertsonian translocation, with higher recurrence risk.

Q: Prader–Willi syndrome results from loss of the:
A) Maternal 15q11–13
B) Paternal 15q11–13
C) Maternal 11p15
D) Paternal 7q11

Q: Flag sign and flaky paint dermatosis are features of:
A) Marasmus
B) Kwashiorkor
C) Pellagra
D) Scurvy
ANS: B
EX: Kwashiorkor (protein deficiency) causes oedema, fatty liver, flaky paint dermatosis and the flag sign of the hair.

Q: Earliest sign of vitamin A deficiency is:
A) Bitot spots
B) Night blindness
C) Keratomalacia
D) Xerophthalmia
ANS: B
EX: Night blindness (nyctalopia) appears first. Bitot spots and keratomalacia come later.

Q: Most radiosensitive cells among the following are:
A) Neurons
B) Lymphocytes
C) Muscle
D) Bone
ANS: B
EX: Lymphocytes are the most radiosensitive. Absolute lymphocyte count at 48 h predicts prognosis.

Q: Perls Prussian blue stain is positive for:
A) Lipofuscin
B) Melanin
C) Hemosiderin
D) Bilirubin
ANS: C
EX: Hemosiderin contains ferric iron, which turns blue with Perls stain. Melanin bleaches with H₂O₂; lipofuscin stains with Sudan black.

Q: Basophilic stippling with microcytic anaemia and wrist drop suggests:
A) Thalassemia
B) Lead poisoning
C) Iron deficiency
D) Arsenic poisoning

C) Complicated plaque
D) Calcified plaque

EX: Fatty streaks (lipid-filled foam cells) are seen even in children under 1 year. They do not obstruct flow.

Q: The key cell responsible for fibrous cap formation in atherosclerosis is:
A) Macrophage
B) Smooth muscle cell
C) Endothelial cell
D) T lymphocyte
ANS: B
EX: SMCs migrate from the media into the intima under PDGF, then proliferate and lay down collagen to form the cap. Foam cells are mostly macrophages.

Q: The plaque in the diagram has a thin fibrous cap over a large necrotic core. Which enzymes from cap macrophages make it likely to rupture?
A) Lipoprotein lipases
B) Matrix metalloproteinases
C) Caspases
D) Lysyl oxidases
ANS: B
EX: Macrophage MMPs break down cap collagen and make the plaque "vulnerable". Statins help stabilise plaques partly by damping this inflammation.

Q: Onion-skin hyperplastic arteriolosclerosis with fibrinoid necrosis is seen in:
A) Benign hypertension
B) Malignant hypertension
C) Diabetes
D) Aging
ANS: B
EX: Malignant hypertension (> 180/120 mmHg) causes necrotizing arteriolitis and a flea-bitten kidney.

Q: The highest risk of free-wall rupture after MI is during:
A) First 24 hours
B) 3–7 days
C) 2–3 weeks
D) 2 months
ANS: B
EX: At 3–7 days, macrophages remove necrotic tissue and the wall is softest, so rupture risk peaks.

Q: Earliest light-microscopic change in myocardial infarction is:
A) Neutrophil infiltration
B) Wavy fibres
C) Granulation tissue
D) Loss of nuclei
ANS: B
EX: Wavy fibres at the border appear within hours. Coagulative necrosis follows at 4–12 h and neutrophils at 12–24 h.

Q: Best marker for diagnosis of re-infarction is:
A) Troponin I
B) CK-MB
C) Myoglobin
D) LDH
ANS: B
EX: CK-MB returns to normal in 48–72 h, so a new rise signals re-infarction. Troponins stay raised for 7–14 days.

Q: Anitschkow (caterpillar) cells are seen in:
A) Infective endocarditis
B) Rheumatic carditis
C) Viral myocarditis
D) Sarcoidosis
ANS: B
EX: Aschoff bodies contain fibrinoid necrosis, T cells and activated macrophages (Anitschkow cells) with wavy ribbon-like chromatin.

Q: Libman–Sacks endocarditis shows vegetations that are:
A) Large and destructive
B) Small, sterile, on both surfaces of valve leaflets
C) Along lines of closure only, warty
D) Bland, loosely attached, in cancer patients
ANS: B
EX: Libman–Sacks (SLE) vegetations are small, sterile and seen on both surfaces. Rheumatic vegetations are warty along closure lines; NBTE are bland vegetations in cancer.

Q: Streptococcus gallolyticus (bovis) endocarditis warrants evaluation for:
A) Gastric carcinoma
B) Colon carcinoma
C) Pancreatic carcinoma
D) Lymphoma
ANS: B
EX: S. gallolyticus bacteraemia is strongly associated with colorectal neoplasia, so colonoscopy is needed.

Q: Most common genetic cause of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is mutation in:

D) Lamin A/C
ANS: B
EX: Sarcomeric MYH7 and MYBPC3 mutations cause HCM, which shows myocyte disarray. Titin is the commonest genetic cause of DCM.

Q: Most common primary tumour of heart in adults is:
A) Rhabdomyoma
B) Myxoma
C) Angiosarcoma
D) Lipoma
ANS: B
EX: Myxoma is usually in the left atrium at the fossa ovalis and can cause ball-valve obstruction. Rhabdomyoma is the commonest in children (tuberous sclerosis).

Q: Pulseless disease in a young Asian woman is:
A) Giant cell arteritis
B) Takayasu arteritis
C) Buerger disease
D) PAN
ANS: B
EX: Takayasu arteritis is granulomatous inflammation of the aortic arch and its branches in women under 40.

ANS: B

A) Takayasu arteritis

D) HSP
ANS: B

Q: Boot-shaped heart is seen in:
A) TGA

D) Coarctation
ANS: B
EX: In TOF, RVH plus a small pulmonary trunk gives the coeur en sabot appearance.
