@ch 13
Q: Leukoplakia is best defined as:
A) Any white lesion of oral mucosa
B) A white patch that cannot be scraped off or characterized as any other disease
C) Candidal infection of mouth
D) Carcinoma in situ
ANS: B
EX: Leukoplakia is a clinical term of exclusion; 3–25% become malignant. Erythroplakia carries a much higher risk.

Q: The most common salivary gland tumour is:
A) Warthin tumour
B) Pleomorphic adenoma
C) Mucoepidermoid carcinoma
D) Adenoid cystic carcinoma
ANS: B
EX: Pleomorphic adenoma is a mixed tumour with chondromyxoid stroma (PLAG1). It recurs after enucleation because of finger-like projections.

Q: Perineural invasion with a cribriform "Swiss-cheese" pattern in a palatal tumour suggests:
A) Pleomorphic adenoma
B) Adenoid cystic carcinoma
C) Acinic cell carcinoma
D) Warthin tumour
ANS: B
EX: Adenoid cystic carcinoma (MYB-NFIB) invades nerves, recurs relentlessly and causes late lung metastases.

Q: Barrett oesophagus is diagnosed histologically by:
A) Squamous hyperplasia
B) Intestinal metaplasia with goblet cells
C) Eosinophils > 15/HPF
D) Gastric cardiac mucosa alone
ANS: B
EX: Goblet cells within columnar mucosa above the GE junction confirm Barrett. It is premalignant for adenocarcinoma.

Q: Most common site of oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma is:
A) Upper third
B) Middle third
C) Lower third
D) GE junction
ANS: B
EX: SCC arises mostly in the middle third. Adenocarcinoma arises in the lower third from Barrett oesophagus.

Q: Autoimmune gastritis characteristically involves:
A) Antrum
B) Body and fundus
C) Duodenum
D) Whole stomach equally
ANS: B
EX: Antibodies against parietal cells and intrinsic factor damage the oxyntic mucosa. This causes achlorhydria, hypergastrinaemia, pernicious anaemia and ECL-cell carcinoids.

Q: Linitis plastica is characteristic of:
A) Intestinal-type gastric carcinoma
B) Diffuse (signet-ring) gastric carcinoma
C) Gastric lymphoma
D) GIST
ANS: B
EX: Diffuse-type carcinoma with CDH1 (E-cadherin) loss infiltrates the wall and gives a rigid "leather bottle" stomach.

Q: GIST arises from:
A) Smooth muscle cells
B) Interstitial cells of Cajal
C) Schwann cells
D) Enterochromaffin cells
ANS: B
EX: GIST is CD117 (KIT) and DOG-1 positive with KIT/PDGFRA mutations, and responds to imatinib. The stomach is the commonest site.

Q: Gastric MALT lymphoma that does NOT regress after H. pylori eradication typically carries:
A) t(14;18)
B) t(11;18) API2-MALT1
C) t(8;14)
D) t(11;14)
ANS: B
EX: t(11;18) MALT lymphomas are resistant to eradication therapy.

Q: The best screening test for coeliac disease is:
A) Anti-gliadin IgG
B) Anti-tissue transglutaminase IgA
C) Anti-endomysial IgG
D) Small bowel biopsy
ANS: B
EX: Anti-tTG IgA is the best screening test. Anti-endomysial is the most specific; biopsy shows increased IELs, crypt hyperplasia and villous atrophy.

Q: PAS-positive foamy macrophages in the lamina propria of small intestine with arthritis suggest:
A) Coeliac disease
B) Whipple disease
C) MAC infection
D) Abetalipoproteinaemia
ANS: B
EX: Tropheryma whipplei produces PAS-positive macrophages that distend the villi. MAC is also PAS positive but is AFB positive.

Q: Transmural inflammation, skip lesions and non-caseating granulomas suggest:
A) Ulcerative colitis
B) Crohn disease
C) Intestinal TB
D) Ischaemic colitis
ANS: B
EX: Crohn disease (NOD2) is transmural with cobblestoning, fistulae and strictures. Smoking worsens it.

Q: Primary sclerosing cholangitis is most associated with:
A) Crohn disease
B) Ulcerative colitis
C) Coeliac disease
D) Whipple disease
ANS: B
EX: About 70% of PSC patients have UC. PSC is p-ANCA positive and carries a risk of cholangiocarcinoma.

Q: The first gene mutated in the adenoma–carcinoma sequence is:
A) KRAS
B) APC
C) TP53
D) SMAD4
ANS: B
EX: APC is the "gatekeeper" (WNT/β-catenin). KRAS, SMAD4/DCC and then TP53 follow.

Q: Hirschsprung disease shows:
A) Hypertrophied ganglion cells
B) Absence of ganglion cells in the narrow distal segment
C) Absence of ganglion cells in the dilated segment
D) Mucosal ulceration
ANS: B
EX: The aganglionic rectal segment is narrow, and the proximal normal colon dilates. RET mutation; Down syndrome association.

Q: Carcinoid syndrome usually requires:
A) Appendiceal primary
B) Hepatic metastases
C) Gastric primary
D) Rectal primary
ANS: B
EX: Serotonin from GI carcinoids is inactivated by the liver. Liver metastases (or a non-portal primary) allow systemic effects with right-sided valve fibrosis.

Q: The most common cause of acute pancreatitis is:
A) Alcohol
B) Gallstones
C) Hypertriglyceridaemia
D) Drugs
ANS: B
EX: Gallstones are commonest overall (alcohol in Indian men). Inappropriate trypsin activation leads to autodigestion and fat necrosis.

Q: The earliest and most frequent mutation in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma is:
A) TP53
B) KRAS
C) SMAD4
D) CDKN2A
ANS: B
EX: KRAS is mutated in > 90%, early in PanIN. CDKN2A (95%), SMAD4 and TP53 follow.

Q: Lesion with a central stellate scar and glycogen-rich clear cuboidal cells in the pancreas of an elderly woman is:
A) Mucinous cystic neoplasm
B) Serous cystadenoma
C) Solid pseudopapillary neoplasm
D) IPMN
ANS: B
EX: Serous cystadenoma (VHL-associated) shows sunburst calcification and is benign.

@ch 14
Q: The most sensitive and specific test for iron deficiency is:
A) Serum iron
B) TIBC
C) Serum ferritin
D) Transferrin saturation
ANS: C
EX: Ferritin is the first to fall and reflects iron stores, although it is raised in inflammation. Marrow iron is the gold standard.

Q: Raised ferritin with low TIBC in a patient with rheumatoid arthritis suggests:
A) Iron deficiency
B) Anaemia of chronic disease
C) Thalassaemia trait
D) Sideroblastic anaemia
ANS: B
EX: IL-6 raises hepcidin, which traps iron in macrophages. Ferritin is high, TIBC low and serum iron low.

Q: Mentzer index < 13 suggests:
A) Iron deficiency
B) β-thalassaemia trait
C) Anaemia of chronic disease
D) Sideroblastic anaemia
ANS: B
EX: In thal trait, MCV is low with a relatively high RBC count. The diagnosis is confirmed by HbA2 > 3.5%.

Q: Earliest peripheral smear finding in megaloblastic anaemia is:
A) Macro-ovalocytes
B) Hypersegmented neutrophils
C) Pancytopenia
D) Howell–Jolly bodies
ANS: B
EX: Neutrophils with 5 or more lobes appear early and are specific.

Q: Neurological manifestations are seen in deficiency of:
A) Folate
B) Vitamin B12
C) Iron
D) Vitamin B6 only
ANS: B
EX: B12 deficiency raises methylmalonic acid, causing subacute combined degeneration of the posterior and lateral columns. Folate deficiency does not.

Q: Increased MCHC is characteristic of:
A) Iron deficiency
B) Hereditary spherocytosis
C) Thalassaemia
D) Megaloblastic anaemia
ANS: B
EX: Membrane loss with dehydration of spherocytes makes HS the main cause of a raised MCHC.

Q: The most sensitive and specific test for hereditary spherocytosis is:
A) Osmotic fragility
B) EMA-binding flow cytometry
C) Coombs test
D) Autohemolysis
ANS: B
EX: Eosin-5-maleimide binding (to band 3) is reduced in HS. Osmotic fragility is the classic test.

Q: Bite cells and Heinz bodies are seen in:
A) Pyruvate kinase deficiency
B) G6PD deficiency
C) HS
D) PNH
ANS: B
EX: Oxidant stress denatures Hb into Heinz bodies, which splenic macrophages "bite" off. G6PD deficiency is X-linked.

Q: The mutation in sickle cell anaemia is:
A) Glu→Lys at β6
B) Glu→Val at β6
C) Val→Glu at β6
D) Deletion of α genes
ANS: B
EX: HbS is β6 Glu→Val; HbC is Glu→Lys. Deoxygenated HbS polymerises.

Q: Most common cause of death in adults with sickle cell disease is:
A) Stroke
B) Acute chest syndrome
C) Splenic sequestration
D) Renal failure
ANS: B
EX: Acute chest syndrome is the leading cause in adults. Sequestration crisis is a danger in young children before autosplenectomy.

Q: HbH inclusions (golf-ball) are seen with deletion of:
A) 1 α gene
B) 2 α genes
C) 3 α genes
D) 4 α genes
ANS: C
EX: Three-gene deletion produces HbH (β4). Four-gene deletion produces Hb Barts and hydrops fetalis.

Q: PNH is due to mutation in:
A) CD55 gene
B) PIGA gene
C) CD59 gene
D) Ankyrin
ANS: B
EX: Acquired PIGA mutation prevents GPI-anchor synthesis, so CD55, CD59 and other anchored proteins are lost. Flow cytometry with FLAER is diagnostic.

Q: Cold agglutinin disease associated with Mycoplasma involves antibody against:
A) Rh antigens
B) I antigen (IgM)
C) P antigen (IgG)
D) i antigen (IgG)
ANS: B
EX: Mycoplasma induces anti-I IgM; EBV induces anti-i. The P antigen is the target of the Donath–Landsteiner antibody in PCH.

Q: Idiosyncratic aplastic anaemia is classically caused by:
A) Busulfan
B) Chloramphenicol
C) Benzene
D) Methotrexate
ANS: B
EX: Chloramphenicol causes dose-independent aplasia (plus dose-dependent suppression). Busulfan and benzene are dose-related causes.

Q: Pure red cell aplasia is associated with:
A) Thymoma
B) Hodgkin lymphoma
C) Myeloma
D) Seminoma
ANS: A
EX: Thymoma, parvovirus B19, Diamond–Blackfan anaemia and LGL leukaemia cause PRCA.

Q: 5q– syndrome is characterised by:
A) Microcytic anaemia, low platelets, poor prognosis
B) Macrocytic anaemia, normal/high platelets, good response to lenalidomide
C) Pancytopenia with Auer rods
D) Ringed sideroblasts and SF3B1
ANS: B
EX: 5q– MDS occurs in elderly women with hypolobated megakaryocytes. It responds to lenalidomide.

@ch 15
Q: Bleeding into joints suggests a defect in:
A) Platelets
B) Coagulation factors
C) Vessel wall
D) vWF only
ANS: B
EX: Coagulation factor defects (haemophilia) cause deep bleeding: haemarthroses and haematomas. Platelet or vessel defects cause mucocutaneous petechiae.

Q: Isolated prolonged aPTT that corrects with mixing, in a boy with haemarthrosis, suggests:
A) vWD type 1
B) Haemophilia A
C) Lupus anticoagulant
D) Factor VII deficiency
ANS: B
EX: Factor VIII deficiency is X-linked. Mixing corrects a factor deficiency but not an inhibitor.

Q: Prolonged aPTT with thrombosis (rather than bleeding) suggests:
A) Haemophilia
B) Antiphospholipid antibodies (lupus anticoagulant)
C) Factor XIII deficiency
D) Vitamin K deficiency
ANS: B
EX: Lupus anticoagulant prolongs aPTT in vitro but causes thrombosis and recurrent miscarriage in vivo.

Q: The most common inherited bleeding disorder is:
A) Haemophilia A
B) von Willebrand disease
C) Glanzmann
D) Bernard–Soulier
ANS: B
EX: vWD affects about 1% of people and is mostly AD type 1. It is treated with desmopressin.

Q: Giant platelets with absent ristocetin-induced aggregation not corrected by normal plasma indicates:
A) vWD
B) Bernard–Soulier syndrome
C) Glanzmann thrombasthenia
D) Storage pool disease
ANS: B
EX: Bernard–Soulier is a GpIb defect. In vWD, ristocetin aggregation corrects with normal plasma. Glanzmann has normal ristocetin but no aggregation with ADP or collagen.

Q: Normal PT, aPTT, platelet count and bleeding time with delayed umbilical stump bleeding suggests:
A) Factor XII deficiency
B) Factor XIII deficiency
C) vWD
D) Haemophilia B
ANS: B
EX: Factor XIII cross-links fibrin and screening tests are normal. Diagnose with the urea clot solubility test.

Q: TTP is due to deficiency of:
A) Factor H
B) ADAMTS13
C) Protein C
D) Antithrombin
ANS: B
EX: Without ADAMTS13, ultra-large vWF multimers accumulate. This gives the pentad of MAHA, thrombocytopenia, neurological signs, renal involvement and fever. Treat with plasma exchange.

Q: HIT is caused by antibodies against:
A) Heparin alone
B) Heparin–platelet factor 4 complex
C) GpIIb/IIIa
D) Antithrombin
ANS: B
EX: IgG against heparin–PF4 activates platelets, causing thrombocytopenia with thrombosis at day 5–14. Switch to argatroban.

Q: Storage temperature of platelet concentrates is:
A) 2–6 °C
B) 20–24 °C with agitation
C) −30 °C
D) −80 °C
ANS: B
EX: Platelets are stored at room temperature with agitation for 5 days. This carries the highest risk of bacterial contamination.

Q: Cryoprecipitate is the richest source of:
A) Factor IX
B) Fibrinogen
C) Factor VII
D) Protein C
ANS: B
EX: Cryoprecipitate contains fibrinogen, factor VIII, vWF, factor XIII and fibronectin. It is used in DIC with low fibrinogen.

Q: The leading cause of transfusion-related mortality is:
A) Febrile non-haemolytic reaction
B) TRALI
C) Allergic reaction
D) Delayed haemolytic reaction
ANS: B
EX: TRALI is caused by donor anti-HLA/HNA antibodies, often from multiparous women. It gives non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema within 6 h.

Q: Irradiation of blood products is done to prevent:
A) Febrile reactions
B) Transfusion-associated GVHD
C) CMV transmission
D) TRALI
ANS: B
EX: Irradiation (25 Gy) inactivates donor T cells. Leukoreduction prevents febrile reactions and CMV transmission.

Q: Bombay blood group lacks:
A) A antigen only
B) H antigen
C) Rh D
D) Kell
ANS: B
EX: Absence of FUT1 means no H substance, so anti-H is present and only Bombay blood can be given.

Q: Anaphylaxis during transfusion in a patient with recurrent sinopulmonary infections suggests:
A) ABO incompatibility
B) IgA deficiency with anti-IgA antibodies
C) TRALI
D) Bacterial contamination
ANS: B
EX: Use washed products or blood from IgA-deficient donors.

@ch 16
Q: Leukaemoid reaction differs from CML by having:
A) Low LAP score
B) High LAP score
C) Philadelphia chromosome
D) Basophilia
ANS: B
EX: LAP (NAP) is high in leukaemoid reaction and low in CML. CML also shows basophilia and BCR-ABL.

Q: Auer rods are seen in:
A) ALL
B) AML
C) CLL
D) Hairy cell leukaemia
ANS: B
EX: Auer rods are MPO-positive needle-like inclusions in myeloblasts. Multiple Auer rods (faggot cells) are seen in APL.

Q: t(15;17) PML-RARA leukaemia is treated with:
A) Imatinib
B) ATRA + arsenic trioxide
C) Rituximab
D) Cladribine
ANS: B
EX: APL presents with DIC and responds to differentiation therapy (ATRA + ATO), giving an excellent prognosis.

Q: Most common translocation in childhood B-ALL with good prognosis is:
A) t(9;22)
B) t(12;21) ETV6-RUNX1
C) t(4;11)
D) t(8;14)
ANS: B
EX: ETV6-RUNX1 and hyperdiploidy are favourable. Ph+ disease, KMT2A rearrangement, hypodiploidy and infancy are unfavourable.

Q: TdT positivity is characteristic of:
A) AML
B) Lymphoblasts (ALL)
C) CLL
D) Myeloma
ANS: B
EX: Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase marks precursor B and T lymphoblasts.

Q: Smudge cells and CD5+, CD23+ B cells indicate:
A) Mantle cell lymphoma
B) CLL
C) Hairy cell leukaemia
D) Follicular lymphoma
ANS: B
EX: CLL is CD5+ and CD23+. Mantle cell lymphoma is CD5+, CD23− and cyclin D1+.

Q: Worst prognostic cytogenetic abnormality in CLL is:
A) del 13q
B) Trisomy 12
C) del 17p (TP53)
D) Normal karyotype
ANS: C
EX: del 17p/TP53 is worst and del 11q (ATM) is poor. Isolated del 13q is favourable.

Q: Hairy cell leukaemia is characterized by all EXCEPT:
A) TRAP positivity
B) BRAF V600E
C) Massive lymphadenopathy
D) Pancytopenia with splenomegaly
ANS: C
EX: Lymphadenopathy is unusual in HCL. Typical features are massive splenomegaly, dry tap, CD11c/CD25/CD103 and cladribine response.

Q: JAK2 V617F is found in over 95% of:
A) CML
B) Polycythaemia vera
C) Essential thrombocythaemia
D) Primary myelofibrosis
ANS: B
EX: PV is almost always JAK2-mutated. ET and PMF carry JAK2 in about 50%, CALR or MPL in the rest.

Q: Teardrop cells with leukoerythroblastic picture and dry tap suggest:
A) CML
B) Primary myelofibrosis
C) Aplastic anaemia
D) PV
ANS: B
EX: Marrow fibrosis forces extramedullary haematopoiesis, giving massive splenomegaly and dacrocytes.

Q: Aquagenic pruritus is classically seen in:
A) CML
B) Polycythaemia vera
C) Hodgkin lymphoma
D) Mastocytosis
ANS: B
EX: Histamine from basophils after warm bathing causes pruritus in PV. Erythromelalgia and thrombosis also occur.

Q: The most common translocation in multiple myeloma is:
A) t(4;14)
B) t(11;14)
C) t(14;16)
D) t(8;14)
ANS: B
EX: t(11;14) (cyclin D1) is the commonest IgH translocation in myeloma. t(4;14) and t(14;16) are high-risk.

Q: Bone scan is often negative in multiple myeloma because:
A) Lesions are too small
B) Lesions are purely lytic with little osteoblastic activity
C) Myeloma spares bones
D) Calcium is low
ANS: B
EX: RANKL up and DKK1 (osteoblast inhibitor) up produce lytic lesions without osteoblastic activity. Use a skeletal survey, MRI or PET.

Q: Hyperviscosity syndrome is most typical of:
A) Multiple myeloma IgG
B) Waldenström macroglobulinaemia
C) MGUS
D) Plasmacytoma
ANS: B
EX: Pentameric IgM causes hyperviscosity. MYD88 L265P; lytic lesions are absent.

Q: MGUS progresses to myeloma at about:
A) 1% per year
B) 10% per year
C) 25% per year
D) Never
ANS: A
EX: MGUS has an M-protein < 3 g/dL, fewer than 10% marrow plasma cells and no CRAB. About 1% progress each year.
