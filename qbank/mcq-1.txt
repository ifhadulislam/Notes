@ch 01
Q: Which of the following ultrastructural changes indicates irreversible cell injury?
A) Swelling of endoplasmic reticulum
B) Small amorphous densities in mitochondria
C) Large flocculent amorphous densities in mitochondria
D) Plasma membrane blebbing
ANS: C
EX: Large flocculent mitochondrial densities (seen in ischemic myocardium) mark the point of no return. ER swelling, blebbing and small densities are reversible changes.

Q: A 45-year-old with long-standing GERD has distal oesophageal biopsy showing columnar epithelium with goblet cells. The process is best described as:
A) Dysplasia
B) Metaplasia
C) Hyperplasia
D) Anaplasia
ANS: B
EX: Barrett oesophagus is intestinal metaplasia: one adult cell type (squamous) is replaced by another (columnar with goblet cells). It is premalignant for adenocarcinoma.

Q: The most characteristic morphological feature of apoptosis is:
A) Cell swelling
B) Chromatin condensation (pyknosis)
C) Inflammation
D) Karyolysis
ANS: B
EX: Chromatin condensation is the most characteristic feature of apoptosis. Cells shrink, the membrane stays intact and there is no inflammation.

Q: Cytochrome c released from mitochondria binds to which molecule to form the apoptosome?
A) FADD
B) APAF-1
C) BCL-2
D) Caspase-8
ANS: B
EX: Cytochrome c + APAF-1 form the apoptosome, which activates caspase-9 (intrinsic pathway). FADD and caspase-8 belong to the extrinsic pathway.

Q: The most reactive (damaging) free radical is:
A) Superoxide anion
B) Hydrogen peroxide
C) Hydroxyl radical
D) Nitric oxide
ANS: C
EX: The hydroxyl radical (OH•), generated for example by the Fenton reaction, is the most reactive species.

Q: Ferroptosis is characterised by all EXCEPT:
A) Iron-dependent lipid peroxidation
B) Loss of GPX4 activity
C) Caspase-3 activation
D) Inhibition by ferrostatin
ANS: C
EX: Ferroptosis is caspase-independent and driven by iron-catalysed lipid peroxidation when GPX4/glutathione fail.

Q: Necroptosis is mediated by:
A) Caspase-1 and gasdermin D
B) RIPK1, RIPK3 and MLKL
C) PARP-1 and AIF
D) Beclin-1 and LC3
ANS: B
EX: Necroptosis is programmed necrosis via RIPK1–RIPK3–MLKL. Caspase-1/gasdermin D = pyroptosis; PARP-1/AIF = parthanatos; Beclin/LC3 = autophagy.

Q: Brown atrophy of the heart in the elderly is due to accumulation of:
A) Hemosiderin
B) Melanin
C) Lipofuscin
D) Bilirubin
ANS: C
EX: Lipofuscin is the "wear-and-tear" pigment formed by lipid peroxidation. It collects in residual bodies, giving brown atrophy.

Q: Fatty change with a single large vacuole displacing the nucleus is NOT typically seen in:
A) Alcoholic liver disease
B) Obesity
C) Reye syndrome
D) Kwashiorkor
ANS: C
EX: Reye syndrome (also acute fatty liver of pregnancy, valproate, tetracycline) causes microvesicular steatosis with a central nucleus.

Q: Most sensitive neurons to ischemia are found in:
A) Purkinje layer of cerebellum
B) CA1 region of hippocampus
C) Layer 4 of neocortex
D) Substantia nigra
ANS: B
EX: CA1 (Sommer sector) pyramidal neurons are the most vulnerable, followed by cerebellar Purkinje cells and neocortical layers 3, 5 and 6.

Q: Werner syndrome, a premature-ageing disorder, results from a defect in:
A) Lamin A
B) DNA helicase (RecQ)
C) Telomerase RNA
D) ATM kinase
ANS: B
EX: Werner syndrome is due to WRN, a RecQ DNA helicase. Lamin A is defective in Hutchinson–Gilford progeria.

Q: Myositis ossificans differs from osteosarcoma in that it shows:
A) Mature bone at the periphery and immature tissue at the centre
B) Immature bone at the periphery
C) Malignant osteoid
D) Codman triangle
ANS: A
EX: Myositis ossificans shows zonation: mature lamellar bone at the periphery and an immature fibroblastic centre. Osteosarcoma shows the reverse pattern.

Q: Which of the following is an example of pure hyperplasia without hypertrophy?
A) Gravid uterus
B) Liver regeneration after partial hepatectomy
C) Left ventricular hypertrophy
D) Skeletal muscle in body builders
ANS: B
EX: Liver regeneration is compensatory hyperplasia. The gravid uterus shows both hyperplasia and hypertrophy; cardiac and skeletal muscle show pure hypertrophy.

@ch 02
Q: Metastatic calcification commonly occurs in all EXCEPT:
A) Gastric mucosa
B) Kidney
C) Lung
D) Atheromatous plaque
ANS: D
EX: Metastatic calcification (hypercalcaemia) targets tissues that excrete acid: the stomach, kidney and lung. Calcification in atheroma is dystrophic.

Q: Psammoma bodies are seen in all EXCEPT:
A) Papillary carcinoma thyroid
B) Serous cystadenocarcinoma ovary
C) Meningioma
D) Follicular carcinoma thyroid
ANS: D
EX: Psammoma bodies are a clue to papillary thyroid carcinoma, serous ovarian tumours, meningioma, mesothelioma and papillary RCC. They are not seen in follicular carcinoma.

Q: Apple-green birefringence under polarized light after Congo red stain is due to:
A) Fibril diameter
B) Cross β-pleated sheet structure
C) P component
D) Glycosaminoglycans
ANS: B
EX: Congo red aligns along the cross-β-pleated sheet fibrils, which produces apple-green birefringence.

Q: Amyloid associated with long-term haemodialysis is derived from:
A) Transthyretin
B) Serum amyloid A
C) β2-microglobulin
D) Immunoglobulin light chain
ANS: C
EX: Aβ2m amyloid follows long-term dialysis because cuprophane membranes do not remove β2-microglobulin. It causes carpal tunnel syndrome and arthropathy.

Q: The most common cause of reactive systemic (AA) amyloidosis in India is:
A) Rheumatoid arthritis
B) Tuberculosis
C) Multiple myeloma
D) Familial Mediterranean fever
ANS: B
EX: Chronic infections, especially tuberculosis, are the commonest cause of AA amyloidosis in India. RA is the commonest in the West; myeloma causes AL amyloid.

Q: "Sago spleen" refers to amyloid deposition in:
A) Red pulp sinusoids
B) Splenic follicles
C) Splenic capsule
D) Trabecular veins
ANS: B
EX: Sago spleen has amyloid in the follicles (tapioca-like granules). A lardaceous spleen has deposits in the red pulp.

Q: Preferred initial site for biopsy in suspected systemic amyloidosis is:
A) Kidney
B) Liver
C) Abdominal fat pad aspirate
D) Heart
ANS: C
EX: Abdominal fat pad aspiration is simple and safe, and is the preferred screening biopsy. Rectal and renal biopsies are alternatives.

Q: Amyloid in medullary carcinoma of thyroid is derived from:
A) Calcitonin
B) Thyroglobulin
C) Amylin
D) Transthyretin
ANS: A
EX: ACal amyloid in medullary thyroid carcinoma is derived from procalcitonin/calcitonin. Amylin (IAPP) amyloid is seen in islets in type 2 diabetes.

Q: Most common cause of death in AL amyloidosis is:
A) Renal failure
B) Cardiac involvement
C) Hepatic failure
D) Bleeding
ANS: B
EX: Cardiac amyloid causes restrictive cardiomyopathy and arrhythmias, and is the commonest cause of death in AL amyloidosis.

Q: Michaelis–Gutmann bodies are characteristic of:
A) Sarcoidosis
B) Malakoplakia
C) Tuberculosis
D) Whipple disease
ANS: B
EX: Malakoplakia shows von Hansemann histiocytes with laminated, calcified, PAS-positive and von Kossa-positive Michaelis–Gutmann bodies.

@ch 03
Q: The hallmark of acute inflammation is:
A) Vasoconstriction
B) Increased vascular permeability
C) Margination
D) Fibrosis
ANS: B
EX: Increased vascular permeability causes the protein-rich exudate that defines acute inflammation.

Q: Rolling of leukocytes on endothelium is mediated by:
A) Integrins
B) Selectins
C) PECAM-1
D) ICAM-1
ANS: B
EX: Selectins (P, E, L) binding sialyl-Lewis X mediate rolling. Integrins with ICAM/VCAM give firm adhesion, and PECAM-1 mediates diapedesis.

Q: Look at the diagram. A child with a congenital defect in fucosylation lacks the leukocyte ligand needed for step 2. The child will have:
A) LAD-1 with delayed cord separation
B) LAD-2 with the Bombay blood group
C) Chronic granulomatous disease
D) Chédiak–Higashi syndrome
ANS: B
EX: Step 2 (rolling) needs selectins to bind sialyl-Lewis X. LAD-2 is a fucosylation defect that removes sialyl-Lewis X and causes the Bombay phenotype. LAD-1 is a CD18 (integrin) defect.

Q: A newborn has delayed separation of the umbilical cord (5 weeks), recurrent bacterial infections without pus, and marked neutrophilia. The defect is in:
A) Sialyl-Lewis X
B) β2 integrin (CD18)
C) NADPH oxidase
D) LYST gene
ANS: B
EX: Leukocyte adhesion deficiency type 1 is a CD18 (β2 integrin) defect. LAD-2 involves sialyl-Lewis X (fucosylation).

Q: Chronic granulomatous disease patients are particularly susceptible to:
A) Catalase-negative organisms
B) Catalase-positive organisms
C) Encapsulated bacteria only
D) Viruses
ANS: B
EX: In CGD, NADPH oxidase is defective. Catalase-positive organisms (S. aureus, Aspergillus, Nocardia, Serratia) destroy their own H₂O₂ and so evade killing.

Q: The most potent bactericidal system of neutrophils is:
A) Lysozyme
B) Lactoferrin
C) H₂O₂–MPO–halide system
D) Major basic protein
ANS: C
EX: Myeloperoxidase converts H₂O₂ and Cl⁻ into hypochlorous acid (HOCl), the most efficient bactericidal system.

Q: Which mediator is chemotactic for neutrophils?
A) LTC4
B) LTB4
C) PGI2
D) Bradykinin
ANS: B
EX: LTB4, C5a, IL-8 and bacterial fMLP are chemotactic. LTC4/D4/E4 cause bronchospasm and increased permeability.

Q: Corticosteroids inhibit arachidonic acid release by inhibiting:
A) Cyclooxygenase
B) 5-lipoxygenase
C) Phospholipase A2
D) Thromboxane synthase
ANS: C
EX: Steroids induce lipocortin (annexin A1), which inhibits phospholipase A2. NSAIDs inhibit COX and zileuton inhibits 5-LO.

Q: Deficiency of C5–C9 predisposes to recurrent infections with:
A) Staphylococcus
B) Neisseria
C) Pneumocystis
D) Candida
ANS: B
EX: Deficiency of the membrane attack complex predisposes to recurrent Neisseria infections. Early classical deficiency gives SLE-like disease; C1-INH deficiency gives hereditary angioedema.

Q: Bread-and-butter pericarditis is an example of:
A) Serous inflammation
B) Fibrinous inflammation
C) Suppurative inflammation
D) Ulcerative inflammation
ANS: B
EX: Fibrinous pericarditis (rheumatic, uraemic, post-MI) looks shaggy, like "bread and butter".

Q: Langhans giant cells are characterised by:
A) Haphazardly scattered nuclei
B) Nuclei arranged in a horseshoe at the periphery
C) Foamy cytoplasm with a central ring of nuclei
D) Owl-eye nucleoli
ANS: B
EX: Langhans giant cells have peripheral horseshoe nuclei (TB, sarcoid). Foreign-body giant cells have scattered nuclei; Touton cells have a ring of nuclei with a foamy rim.

Q: The key cytokine that converts macrophages into epithelioid cells in a granuloma is:
A) IL-4
B) IFN-γ
C) IL-10
D) TGF-β
ANS: B
EX: Th1-derived IFN-γ activates macrophages, which become epithelioid cells. TNF maintains the granuloma, so anti-TNF therapy reactivates TB.

Q: Stellate (suppurative) granulomas are characteristic of:
A) Sarcoidosis
B) Cat-scratch disease
C) Crohn disease
D) Berylliosis
ANS: B
EX: Cat-scratch disease (Bartonella henselae), LGV and tularaemia produce stellate, necrotizing, suppurative granulomas.

Q: Which of the following is a negative acute phase reactant?
A) Fibrinogen
B) Ferritin
C) Albumin
D) Hepcidin
ANS: C
EX: Albumin, transferrin and transthyretin fall during the acute phase. Fibrinogen, ferritin, hepcidin, CRP and SAA rise.

Q: Fibrin-ring (doughnut) granulomas are classically seen in:
A) Q fever
B) Tuberculosis
C) Leprosy
D) Schistosomiasis
ANS: A
EX: Fibrin-ring granulomas in the liver or marrow are classic for Q fever (Coxiella burnetii). They are also reported with Hodgkin lymphoma, CMV, EBV and allopurinol.

@ch 04
Q: The most important growth factor for angiogenesis is:
A) PDGF
B) VEGF
C) TGF-β
D) EGF
ANS: B
EX: VEGF, induced by hypoxia through HIF-1α, is the most important angiogenic factor.

Q: The most important fibrogenic cytokine is:
A) TNF
B) TGF-β
C) IL-1
D) FGF
ANS: B
EX: TGF-β stimulates collagen and fibronectin synthesis and inhibits MMPs. It drives scarring in wounds and in organ fibrosis.

Q: Tensile strength of a skin wound at the end of 3 months reaches approximately:
A) 10%
B) 30%
C) 70–80%
D) 100%
ANS: C
EX: Wound strength is about 10% at one week and plateaus at 70–80% by 3 months. It never fully regains normal strength.

Q: Collagen bridging the incision line in primary union occurs by:
A) Day 1
B) Day 3
C) Day 5
D) Week 4
ANS: C
EX: By day 5, neovascularization is maximal and collagen fibrils bridge the incision. On day 3, collagen fibres are vertical and do not yet bridge.

Q: A keloid differs from a hypertrophic scar in that a keloid:
A) Stays within wound margins
B) Regresses spontaneously
C) Extends beyond the wound margins and recurs
D) Lacks collagen
ANS: C
EX: Keloids grow beyond the original wound margins, do not regress and recur after excision. They are common in people of African descent.

Q: Stem cells of the intestine reside in:
A) Villous tips
B) Base of crypts
C) Brunner glands
D) Lamina propria
ANS: B
EX: Lgr5+ intestinal stem cells sit at the base of the crypts. Skin stem cells sit in the hair-follicle bulge and limbal stem cells in the cornea.

Q: Lysyl oxidase, needed for collagen cross-linking, requires:
A) Zinc
B) Copper
C) Vitamin C
D) Iron
ANS: B
EX: Lysyl oxidase is copper-dependent (defective in Menkes disease). Vitamin C is needed for proline and lysine hydroxylation.

Q: Healing of a CNS injury occurs predominantly by:
A) Fibroblast proliferation
B) Gliosis by astrocytes
C) Neuronal regeneration
D) Microglial scarring
ANS: B
EX: The CNS has no significant fibroblast response. Repair is by astrocytic gliosis (gemistocytes).

Q: Rate of axonal regeneration after peripheral nerve injury is about:
A) 0.1 mm/day
B) 1–3 mm/day
C) 1 cm/day
D) 5 cm/day
ANS: B
EX: Axons regrow at roughly 1–3 mm a day along Schwann-cell bands of Büngner. Misdirected growth forms a traumatic neuroma.

Q: The most important local cause of delayed wound healing is:
A) Foreign body
B) Infection
C) Mechanical stress
D) Poor blood supply
ANS: B
EX: Infection is the single most important cause of delayed wound healing.

@ch 05
Q: Lines of Zahn indicate:
A) Postmortem clot
B) Thrombus formed in flowing blood
C) Embolus
D) Infarct
ANS: B
EX: Alternating pale platelet–fibrin layers and dark RBC layers form only in flowing blood, which identifies an antemortem thrombus.

Q: The most common inherited cause of hypercoagulability is:
A) Protein C deficiency
B) Antithrombin III deficiency
C) Factor V Leiden
D) Prothrombin G20210A
ANS: C
EX: Factor V Leiden (Arg506Gln) causes resistance to activated protein C. It is the commonest inherited thrombophilia.

Q: Nutmeg liver is due to:
A) Left ventricular failure
B) Right heart failure (chronic passive congestion)
C) Portal vein thrombosis
D) Fatty change
ANS: B
EX: Chronic passive congestion causes red-brown congested centrilobular areas that alternate with pale periportal areas, giving the nutmeg look.

Q: Heart failure cells are:
A) Hemosiderin-laden alveolar macrophages
B) Hypertrophied myocytes
C) Lipofuscin-laden myocytes
D) Fibroblasts in lung
ANS: A
EX: In chronic congestion of the lung (left heart failure, mitral stenosis), alveolar macrophages fill with hemosiderin. These "heart failure cells" are Perls positive.

Q: White (anaemic) infarcts occur in all EXCEPT:
A) Heart
B) Spleen
C) Kidney
D) Lung
ANS: D
EX: The lung, with its dual blood supply and loose tissue, gets red (haemorrhagic) infarcts. Solid organs with end-arteries get white infarcts.

Q: A patient develops dyspnoea, confusion and a petechial rash over the axillae 2 days after a femoral fracture. The likely diagnosis is:
A) Pulmonary thromboembolism
B) Fat embolism syndrome
C) Air embolism
D) Amniotic fluid embolism
ANS: B
EX: Fat embolism appears 1–3 days after long-bone fracture with respiratory insufficiency, neurological signs, anaemia, thrombocytopenia and petechiae.

Q: In amniotic fluid embolism, the pulmonary vasculature shows:
A) Fat globules only
B) Fetal squamous cells, lanugo hair and mucin
C) Air bubbles
D) Tumour cells
ANS: B
EX: Fetal squames, lanugo, vernix fat and mucin are found in the maternal pulmonary circulation. Mortality is about 80%, and DIC is common.

Q: The most common cause of DIC is:
A) Acute promyelocytic leukaemia
B) Gram-negative sepsis
C) Abruptio placentae
D) Snake bite
ANS: B
EX: Sepsis (Gram-negative LPS → tissue factor) is the commonest cause. Obstetric causes and APL are classic but less frequent.

Q: The most specific laboratory test for DIC among the following is:
A) Prolonged PT
B) Thrombocytopenia
C) Raised D-dimer
D) Low fibrinogen
ANS: C
EX: D-dimer reflects fibrinolysis of cross-linked fibrin and is the most useful single marker in DIC.

Q: In shock, the kidney typically shows:
A) Acute cortical necrosis
B) Acute tubular necrosis
C) Papillary necrosis
D) Glomerulonephritis
ANS: B
EX: Ischaemic ATN is the commonest renal lesion in shock. Cortical necrosis is mostly obstetric (abruptio).

Q: Gamna–Gandy bodies are found in:
A) Congestive splenomegaly
B) Amyloidosis
C) Hodgkin lymphoma
D) Malaria
ANS: A
EX: Siderofibrotic nodules (iron + calcium on collagen) form in chronic congestive splenomegaly (portal hypertension) and sickle cell disease.

Q: The earliest change in septic shock pathogenesis is driven mainly by:
A) Hypovolemia
B) TNF and IL-1 release after TLR stimulation
C) Myocardial pump failure
D) IgE-mediated mast cell degranulation
ANS: B
EX: PAMPs such as LPS activate TLRs. The resulting TNF, IL-1 and IL-6 drive vasodilation, capillary leak and DIC.

Q: Paradoxical embolism requires:
A) Saddle embolus
B) Interatrial or interventricular communication
C) Atrial fibrillation
D) Atheroma
ANS: B
EX: A venous embolus crosses into the systemic circulation through an ASD, PFO or VSD.

@ch 06
Q: The single most reliable feature distinguishing malignant from benign tumours is:
A) Anaplasia
B) Rapid growth
C) Metastasis
D) Necrosis
ANS: C
EX: Metastasis unequivocally marks a tumour as malignant. Some benign tumours grow fast and some malignant ones are well differentiated.

Q: A hamartoma is:
A) Normal tissue at an abnormal site
B) Disorganized mature tissue indigenous to the site
C) Tumour of all three germ layers
D) Malignant tumour of blood vessels
ANS: B
EX: Example: a pulmonary hamartoma with cartilage (popcorn calcification). A choristoma is normal tissue at a wrong site.

Q: HPV E7 protein promotes carcinogenesis by:
A) Degrading p53
B) Binding and inactivating RB
C) Activating telomerase only
D) Mimicking CD40
ANS: B
EX: E7 binds RB and releases E2F. E6 degrades p53. CD40 mimicry is EBV LMP-1.

Q: Li–Fraumeni syndrome is due to germline mutation in:
A) RB1
B) TP53
C) APC
D) PTEN
ANS: B
EX: Germline TP53 mutation predisposes to sarcomas, breast cancer, brain tumours, adrenocortical carcinoma and leukaemia.

Q: Which carcinogen is associated with angiosarcoma of the liver?
A) Aflatoxin
B) Vinyl chloride
C) Benzene
D) β-naphthylamine
ANS: B
EX: Vinyl chloride, arsenic and Thorotrast cause hepatic angiosarcoma. Aflatoxin causes HCC; benzene causes AML; naphthylamine causes bladder cancer.

Q: The hallmark of a tumour suppressor gene is that:
A) One mutant allele is sufficient
B) Both alleles must be inactivated (two-hit)
C) It is always amplified
D) It encodes a growth factor
ANS: B
EX: Knudson's two-hit hypothesis states that loss of both alleles is needed. Oncogenes act dominantly.

Q: Hypercalcaemia in squamous cell carcinoma of the lung is due to:
A) Bone metastasis
B) PTHrP
C) Calcitriol
D) Ectopic PTH
ANS: B
EX: Squamous cell carcinoma secretes PTH-related peptide. Lymphomas can make calcitriol; true ectopic PTH is rare.

Q: Lambert–Eaton syndrome is most commonly associated with:
A) Thymoma
B) Small cell carcinoma lung
C) Squamous cell carcinoma lung
D) Ovarian teratoma
ANS: B
EX: Antibodies to presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channels are seen with SCLC. Thymoma is linked with myasthenia gravis; ovarian teratoma with anti-NMDA encephalitis.

Q: A tumour composed of cells positive for CD99 and FLI-1 in a 12-year-old's femoral diaphysis is most likely:
A) Osteosarcoma
B) Ewing sarcoma
C) Lymphoma
D) Neuroblastoma
ANS: B
EX: Ewing sarcoma shows membranous CD99 and FLI-1 positivity. It has t(11;22) EWSR1-FLI1.

Q: Which marker is most specific for rhabdomyosarcoma?
A) Desmin
B) Myogenin
C) Vimentin
D) SMA
ANS: B
EX: Myogenin and MyoD1 are skeletal-muscle transcription factors and are the most specific markers. Desmin is also found in smooth muscle.

Q: Warburg effect refers to:
A) Increased oxidative phosphorylation
B) Aerobic glycolysis in cancer cells
C) Increased fatty acid oxidation
D) Glutamine starvation
ANS: B
EX: Cancer cells use glycolysis even when oxygen is plentiful. This is the basis of FDG-PET imaging.

Q: The most important prognostic indicator of a malignant tumour is generally:
A) Grade
B) Stage
C) Tumour marker level
D) Patient age
ANS: B
EX: Stage (TNM) has greater prognostic value than grade.

Q: N-MYC amplification is a poor prognostic factor in:
A) Burkitt lymphoma
B) Neuroblastoma
C) Small cell carcinoma
D) Wilms tumour
ANS: B
EX: N-MYC amplification in neuroblastoma predicts poor outcome. C-MYC is involved in Burkitt and L-MYC in SCLC.

Q: Seed-and-soil hypothesis of metastasis was proposed by:
A) Virchow
B) Paget
C) Knudson
D) Warburg
ANS: B
EX: Stephen Paget (1889) proposed that tumour cells (seeds) colonise only congenial organs (soil).

Q: Trousseau syndrome (migratory thrombophlebitis) is classically associated with:
A) Renal cell carcinoma
B) Pancreatic carcinoma
C) Prostate carcinoma
D) Thyroid carcinoma
ANS: B
EX: Mucin-secreting adenocarcinomas, especially of the pancreas, release procoagulants that cause migratory thrombophlebitis.
